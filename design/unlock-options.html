Kinfold · key management

Twenty bits

Can a member unlock their family tree with six digits, the way a banking app does? Not on its own — but the reason why points at the design that actually works.

The problem

Six digits is a millionth of the search space you need

A six-digit PIN has exactly 1,000,000 possible values — about 20 bits of entropy. The keys protecting the tree are 256-bit. If the PIN is the key, an attacker holding the encrypted file simply tries all million and stops when one decrypts cleanly.

Slow key-stretching helps, and Kinfold already uses PBKDF2 at 600,000 iterations. Measured on one CPU core, that is 92 ms per guess — a million guesses would take a day. But an attacker does not use one CPU core.

6-digit PIN20 bits · ~2 min on one GPU
Six-word passphrase62 bits · centuries
Kinfold recovery phrase128 bits · not happening
A slice key256 bits

A single consumer graphics card runs this key-derivation roughly four orders of magnitude faster than the measurement above. The whole six-digit space falls in about two minutes. Not a theoretical margin — an afternoon's work with off-the-shelf hardware.

The insight

Your bank's PIN isn't protecting your money — the attempt counter is

A cash machine takes four digits because it takes your card away after three wrong tries. Your phone takes six because a chip refuses to answer faster than once a second and wipes itself after ten. In neither case is the number doing the work. The thing that counts wrong answers is doing the work.

So the real question is not "how many digits?" It is: can the attacker guess in private, or must they ask someone who is keeping score?

Six digits are safe exactly when something outside the attacker's control is counting wrong answers — and dangerous the moment the secret can be attacked on their own hardware.

B

Six digits, on this device only

Recommended

123 456 789

The private key is generated in the browser and stored in IndexedDB as a non-extractable key. The browser will use it but will not hand out its bytes — not to our code, not to anyone with a copy of the database file. The PIN is a lock on the app, not on the cryptography.

This is the honest version of what you asked for, and it is genuinely good: an attacker who steals the server gets nothing at all, because the key was never there.

Strength
Strong against the server being breached. Moderate against someone holding your unlocked phone.
Cost
Nothing. Pure browser APIs.
Catch
The key lives on that one device. A new phone needs a fresh grant from the owner, or the phrase from A.
Verdict
Ship this. It is the everyday experience.
C

Passkey — face or fingerprint

Recommended
Unlock with Face ID
No PIN, no phrase. The key is derived from the passkey itself.

WebAuthn's PRF extension lets a passkey produce a stable 32-byte secret. That secret unwraps the slice keys. The member touches a sensor and the tree opens — and the key material is held by the phone's secure hardware, which enforces its own attempt limits.

Better UX and better security than six digits, which is a rare combination and the reason to prefer it.

Strength
Highest of the four. Hardware-backed, phishing-resistant.
Cost
Nothing.
Catch
Support is good but not universal — solid on Android, macOS 15+, iOS 18.4+, Windows 11; absent on Firefox for Android. Treat it as an upgrade, never the only path.
Verdict
Offer it first, fall back to B.
D

Six digits that work on any device

Later, if ever
Enter your PIN
2 attempts remaining before this key is destroyed

The Signal model. The PIN is stretched, and the result unlocks a request to a service holding 256 random bits. Get it wrong five times and that service deletes its share — the tree becomes permanently unreadable to everyone, including you.

The trap: the counter has to be something the operator cannot bypass. Signal uses secure enclaves. Without one, you hold both the counter and the encrypted file, which means you could brute-force it — and you are right back to being the custodian you were trying to stop being.

Strength
Excellent with an enclave or split across two independent providers. Illusory on a single server you control.
Cost
Real money, or real complexity. Threshold schemes need at least two operators who do not collude.
Catch
It reintroduces exactly the custody problem the encryption removed, unless done properly.
Verdict
Do not build this now. Revisit only if cross-device recovery becomes the thing blocking adoption.
Recommendation

Layer three of them and never build the fourth

Each layer catches what the one above it drops. The member meets the simplest thing their device supports, and only ever sees the recovery phrase on the day they set up or the day something has gone wrong.

  1. Passkey, where it works

    Face or fingerprint. Nothing to remember, nothing to type, hardware enforcing its own limits.

  2. Six-digit PIN over a non-extractable device key

    The fallback on every other browser, and the answer to your original question. The digits gate the app; the browser refuses to surrender the key itself.

  3. Recovery phrase, shown once

    New phone, lost passkey, forgotten PIN. Also what an owner uses to re-grant a member who has lost everything.

This costs nothing, needs no server you have to be trusted with, and gives most members a one-touch unlock. The six digits you asked for are in there — sitting on top of a key that is never transmitted, rather than pretending to be one.

Timings measured with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 at 600,000 iterations on a single cloud CPU core (92 ms per derivation); the GPU figure is an estimate scaled from published hashcat throughput for the same function and should be treated as an order of magnitude, not a benchmark.
